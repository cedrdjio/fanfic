# Chapitre 36 — Assimilation

After class on Monday, Shuichi went to the Forest of Death.

He did not take the usual route. After leaving the school, he followed the upper reaches of the Naka River, turned north at the stone bridge, passed through the pine forest on the edge of the Training Ground, and entered through the western entrance of the Forest of Death. This route was twice as long as starting from the Uchiha Clan grounds, but it allowed him to avoid anyone he might encounter there.

Lue did not follow. Shuichi let it stay in the plum tree to rest ; there was no need for scouting today.

The burning sensation in his palm had persisted for two days. Sakumo Hatake’s combat experience was burning quietly within the mental barrier, like a wrapped ball of flame. Shuichi could feel the barrier continuously consuming mental energy to maintain it ; the consumption wasn’t large, but it would accumulate over time. He needed to start assimilating it as soon as possible.

Shuichi walked deep into the Forest of Death and found the east side of the pine forest where he had hunted the bear last time. There was a flat area surrounded by pine trees, covered in a thick layer of pine needles that were soft and silent underfoot. Shuichi used his Sharingan to scan the surrounding environment, confirming that there were no traces of anyone else in the pine forest. Then, he closed his Sharingan and sat quietly for about a minute, listening with his ears—the sound of the wind through the pine needles, the sound of the river in the distance, and the occasional sound of a bird taking off from a branch. There were no footsteps of people, no subtle fluctuations of Chakra flow.

He closed his eyes and focused his attention on the palm of his right hand.

The mental barrier in his palm pulsed slightly, like a heart being pressed. Shuichi took a deep breath and began to release the barrier.

The moment the barrier loosened, Sakumo’s combat experience gushed out like a broken levee.

Shuichi’s head felt like it had been hit by a hammer.

It wasn’t pain ; it was fullness. Too much information flooded in at once—visual, tactile, auditory, olfactory ; decades of combat memories exploded in Shuichi’s mind simultaneously. He saw the scene of Sakumo performing his first B-rank mission at twenty, saw the scene of Sakumo fighting a Jonin from Iwagakure in the rainforest at twenty-five, and saw the entire process of Sakumo leading a team on an S-rank mission at thirty. Every memory carried complete sensory details—the resistance when the blade cut into an enemy’s ribs, the warmth of Chakra flowing through meridians, the stinging sensation of battlefield smoke entering his nasal cavity.

Shuichi gritted his teeth and used his Sharingan to help sort it out.

The two-tomoe Sharingan spun violently at the edge of his pupils, breaking down the incoming combat experience into identifiable templates. Close-quarters combat, Ninjutsu combinations, tactical judgment, battlefield intuition, opponent analysis, terrain utilization, team cooperation—every neural pathway was dismantled by the Sharingan into independent information blocks, then arranged by category.

This process lasted about a quarter of an hour.

When Shuichi opened his eyes, beads of sweat on his forehead slid down his cheeks and dripped onto the pine-needle-covered ground. His breathing was heavy, like he had just finished a long run. His palms were trembling, and his knuckles were white from clenching his fists too tightly. His temples throbbed, as if something was knocking against the inside of his skull.

The chaos in his mind had subsided. Sakumo’s combat experience had been organized into orderly information blocks, quietly arranged in his memory. Close-quarters combat was at the forefront, like an open manual of swordsmanship, every page filled with techniques Sakumo had verified with blood on the battlefield. Ninjutsu combinations were ranked second ; the way Lightning Release and Wind Release coordinated was like a set of precise gears, with every part interlocking seamlessly. Tactical judgment was third ; it wasn’t text or images, but a way of thinking—being able to judge the direction of the center of gravity just by seeing an opponent’s stance, or choosing the optimal position just by seeing the slope of the ground. Battlefield intuition was last—fuzzy, uncertain, like a shadow in the fog, needing actual combat to see its outline clearly.

Shuichi moved his fingers and stood up.

He needed to verify the knowledge he had just acquired.

Shuichi took a Kunai out of his Ninja tool pouch.

He stood in the center of the flat ground holding the Kunai, closed his eyes, and retrieved Sakumo’s close-quarters combat template from his memory.

Sakumo’s close-quarters combat was centered on a short blade. White Light was a short blade, with a blade length of one foot and two inches—longer than a Kunai, shorter than a katana. This length determined Sakumo’s combat style—not long-range throwing, nor wide, sweeping slashes, but rapid cutting in at medium range. One step in, blade enters ; one step back, blade out. The three actions combined took less than a second.

Shuichi opened his eyes and stepped forward.

The Kunai cut into the air diagonally from below ; the angle and force came from Sakumo’s memory. At the moment of entry, Shuichi’s wrist turned slightly, changing the angle of the blade from a diagonal cut to a horizontal slash—this was Sakumo’s habitual feint ; he didn’t pull the blade out after entering, but flipped the blade to slash horizontally, turning one knife mark into two.

Shuichi’s wrist jammed during the flip.

The movement was broken. The angle of the horizontal slash was off, and the trajectory the blade traced was two inches shorter than in Sakumo’s memory. Shuichi stopped and looked at his own wrist. The flipping motion wasn’t fluid because his wrist flexibility was different from Sakumo’s. Sakumo had practiced with short blades for over twenty years ; his wrist’s range of motion and muscle memory were different from Shuichi’s.

Shuichi readjusted, comparing Sakumo’s memory with his own physical condition.

Sakumo was about one meter eighty tall, with an arm span one-third longer than Shuichi’s. Shuichi was eight years old, one meter thirty tall, and his arm span was much shorter. Sakumo’s entry distance was one step plus one arm length plus one blade length ; Shuichi’s entry distance was at least thirty centimeters shorter. This meant many of Sakumo’s close-quarters techniques were temporarily unusable by Shuichi—he couldn’t reach.

But the core logic of the swordsmanship could be learned.

Sakumo’s short blade technique had three core principles : First, the entry angle should always be from bottom to top or a diagonal cut from the side, never a frontal hack. Frontal hacks have the most power but are the easiest to predict ; diagonal cuts have many angles and change quickly, making it hard for the opponent to judge the direction of the next strike. Second, after every entry, a follow-up move must be connected ; do not stay in the same position for more than a second. In Sakumo’s combat records, the average interval between continuous attacks was 0.7 seconds, with the shortest interval being 0.3 seconds. Third, footsteps and the blade must be synchronized ; advance with the blade, retreat with the blade ; the blade and feet must always be in the same rhythm.

Shuichi closed his eyes, re-loaded Sakumo’s combat template, culled the parts unsuitable for his own physical condition, and kept the core principles and adjustable techniques.

Then he opened his eyes and stepped forward again.

This time, he didn’t copy Sakumo’s movements exactly. Based on his one-meter-thirty height and shorter arm span, he adjusted the entry distance and angle. His stride was smaller, the entry angle was more toward the side, and he utilized the advantage of a low center of gravity to cut in from below.

The Kunai cut through the air diagonally from the side, his wrist flipped, and he slashed horizontally. The movement was smoother than the first time, though still not fluid enough. The angle of the flip was correct, but the speed was slow. In Sakumo’s memory, this flip only took 0.2 seconds ; Shuichi took nearly 0.5 seconds.

Shuichi practiced repeatedly ten times. By the tenth time, the speed of the flip was faster, but it was still a bit off from Sakumo’s memory. This wasn’t a problem of memory ; it was a problem of muscles. Shuichi’s wrist hadn’t undergone twenty years of short blade training ; his muscle memory couldn’t keep up with the speed of nerve signal transmission.

But it was much stronger than three days ago.

What was the level of Shuichi’s close-quarters combat three days ago ? Chunin. He knew how to hold a blade, knew the basic entry angles, knew that footsteps should be synchronized with the blade. But this knowledge stopped at « knowing, » not « doing, » and definitely not being as familiar as he was now. Knowing that the entry angle should be a diagonal cut from the side was one thing ; completing the entry plus the follow-up move within 0.7 seconds in actual combat was another.

Now, Sakumo’s memory filled this gap.

Shuichi didn’t need to grope around to figure out how many degrees the entry angle should be biased, how many fractions of a second earlier the timing of the follow-up move should be, or how the rhythm of the footsteps and the blade should be coordinated. Sakumo had tested all these details over twenty-plus years of actual combat, and the optimal solution was already carved into his neural pathways. Shuichi only needed to copy these optimal solutions and then make adjustments based on his own physical condition.

This was the core value of Plunder combat experience—not directly gaining combat power, but saving the process of exploration.

Shuichi stood on the flat ground in the pine forest, the Kunai flipping in his hand. He practiced the cycle of entry, follow-up move, and retreating repeatedly, adjusting the details each time. His palm was rubbed red by the handle of the Kunai, but he didn’t stop.

After practicing for about half an hour, Shuichi stopped and moved his wrist.

The cycle of entry plus follow-up move was already relatively smooth, and the speed had been reduced from the initial 0.5 seconds to about 0.3 seconds. There was still a gap from Sakumo’s 0.2 seconds, but the speed of progress surprised even Shuichi himself.

Half an hour. From 0.5 seconds to 0.3 seconds, half an hour.

Shuichi thought about his training over the past three years.

Three years. From five to eight years old, in three years, Shuichi went from fighting wild rabbits to bears in the Forest of Death, from practicing standing postures to footwork coordination in the Training Ground, and from Chakra refining to Fire Release control in the backyard. Three years of accumulation had taken Shuichi from an ordinary person to a Chunin level.

But compared to Sakumo’s memory, the gap in close-quarters combat at the Chunin level was significant. It wasn’t that Shuichi hadn’t worked hard enough, but that there was a huge gap of experience between three years of fumbling and twenty years of actual combat. Shuichi knew the entry angle should be a diagonal cut from the side, but he didn’t know the optimal angle for a diagonal cut from the side was thirty-seven degrees, not forty-five. He knew the follow-up move should be fast, but he didn’t know that 0.2 seconds after entering was the best time for a follow-up move, not 0.3 seconds. Every one of these details could decide life and death.

Sakumo’s memory filled in all these details.

Shuichi stood on the flat ground in the pine forest, looking at the Kunai in his hand. His palm was red, his wrist was sore, but there was an indescribable feeling in his heart.

It felt like he had received a windfall.

Three years of bitter training, step by step from wild rabbits to bears, from standing posture to footwork coordination, from Chunin to... still Chunin. Then Sakumo died, and Shuichi spent fifteen seconds plundering his combat experience, spent half an hour digesting it, and his close-quarters combat level jumped by nearly a whole tier.

Three years and half an hour. This contrast was too stark.

Shuichi took a deep breath and put away his Kunai.

He knew this feeling was dangerous. Easy money came too easily, and it could make someone dependent on it.

Furthermore, such opportunities were extremely rare. Sakumo was a Kage-level powerhouse with incredibly rich combat experience, and his time of death was within Shuichi’s control. The probability of all these conditions being met simultaneously was far too low. Most powerhouses' deaths would not be within Shuichi’s control—deaths on the battlefield were too chaotic, leaving no time for Plunder ; those who died of natural causes had already cooled neural pathways, making it impossible to Plunder complete experience. Sakumo’s case was the result of a perfect alignment between Shuichi’s memories of his past life and real-world conditions.

There wouldn’t be a second time for this kind of easy money.

Shuichi kept this in mind, then turned his attention back to digesting the combat experience.

---

Close-quarters combat was only a part of Sakumo’s combat experience.

Shuichi sat back down on the pine-needle-covered ground, closed his eyes, and pulled up Sakumo’s Ninjutsu combination template.

Sakumo’s Chakra natures were Lightning and Wind. Lightning Release was his primary nature, and Wind Release was his auxiliary nature. His Ninjutsu combinations were centered around Lightning Release, with Wind Release assisting in acceleration and expanding the attack range. His most classic combination was a Wind Release acceleration followed by a Lightning Release thrust—Wind Release compressed the air to create propulsion, boosting Sakumo’s movement speed to the level of the Body Flicker Technique, and then Lightning Release condensed on his short blade, piercing through in a single strike.

Shuichi carefully sensed Sakumo’s memories of his Ninjutsu combinations.

Then, he gave up.

The Chakra swirled in his palm, warm and still possessing the properties of fire. It wasn’t that Shuichi couldn’t achieve Lightning nature transformation ; Chakra nature transformations could be trained later on, but it took time, and the poorer the affinity, the slower the training. Shuichi had tested his own affinities and found them to be Fire, Water, and Earth, with Lightning and Wind ranking at the bottom.

If he spent a lot of time training in Lightning Release and Wind Release now, his Fire Release training would be put on hold. Fire was the natural affinity of the Uchiha Clan. Shuichi had only just scratched the surface after a month of practice ; if he switched to Lightning and Wind now, his progress in Fire would be completely ruined. It would be putting the cart before the horse.

There was no rush in terms of time. When the Third Shinobi World War broke out, a large number of shinobi with different natures would die on the battlefield. By then, Shuichi could directly obtain the Lightning and Wind Chakra nature transformations through Plunder, which would be more than ten times faster than training them later. Sakumo’s Wind-Lightning combination memories could be kept ; once Shuichi obtained the Wind and Lightning natures, they would serve as a ready-made reference template.

Shuichi removed Sakumo’s Ninjutsu combination template from his digestion queue. This part of the experience was useless to him for now, but he did not discard it—in the future, Shuichi would gradually Plunder other Chakra nature transformations, eventually possessing all five natures. When that time came, Sakumo’s Wind-Lightning combination memories would be ready-made knowledge.

He turned his attention to Sakumo’s tactical judgment and battlefield intuition.

This part was independent of Chakra natures.

Sakumo’s tactical judgment had four core modules : opponent analysis, terrain utilization, timing selection, and retreat evaluation. Each module was a summary of countless real battles, not theoretical deductions, but experience truly bought with his life on the battlefield.

Shuichi pulled up the opponent analysis module. The way Sakumo observed his opponents in battle was completely different from Shuichi’s. Shuichi relied on the Sharingan to predict the opponent’s movement trajectory, which was a visual-level prediction. Sakumo relied on comprehensive judgment—the opponent’s posture, breathing frequency, the amplitude of Chakra fluctuations, subtle finger movements, and shifts in the foot’s center of gravity. All of this information was processed simultaneously in Sakumo’s mind, forming a judgment within 0.3 seconds.

When Shuichi didn’t have the Sharingan’s predictive ability, he could only rely on visual observation. However, Sakumo’s comprehensive judgment method could be used even without the Sharingan. Shuichi tried to observe his surroundings using Sakumo’s method—the spacing of the pine trees, the slope of the ground, the direction of the wind blowing through the pine needles. The amount of information was several times larger than simply predicting with the Sharingan, but his processing speed couldn’t keep up. Sakumo’s brain could process over a dozen sensory inputs simultaneously, while Shuichi’s could only handle five or six.

The gap lay in brainpower, not memory. Memory could be plundered, but brainpower could only be trained.

But at least Shuichi now knew the direction. Previously, he didn’t know what exactly to observe for « comprehensive judgment, » but now Sakumo’s memory had laid out the observation checklist. All that was left was to practice.

Shuichi then pulled up the terrain utilization module. Sakumo’s utilization of terrain was extremely precise. Before a battle began, he would spend three seconds scanning the surrounding environment, memorizing all usable terrain features—elevation differences, obstacles, light direction, and wind direction. Then, he would use these features to create an advantage during the fight. For example, positioning himself with his back to the light so the opponent’s view was obscured by the glare, using elevation differences to change his angle of entry, or forcing the opponent into a narrow space to restrict their movement.

These terrain utilization techniques were completely unrestricted by Chakra natures. Shuichi could use them right now.

He stood up and simulated Sakumo’s terrain-scanning method on the flat ground of the pine forest. Three seconds to scan the surroundings—on the east side of the flat ground, two pine trees stood close together ; on the west side, there was a protruding rock ; to the north was an uphill slope, and to the south was a downhill slope. If an opponent came from the north, the uphill slope would slow them down, so Shuichi should stand on the south side to utilize the height difference. If the opponent came from the east, the narrow space between the two pine trees could restrict their dodging space.

In his mind, Shuichi simulated three different opponent directions, adjusting his positioning for each based on Sakumo’s terrain utilization principles. Previously, he wouldn’t have thought of these things, but now Sakumo’s memory made these judgments instinctive.

Battlefield intuition was the hardest part of Sakumo’s combat experience to digest.

Battlefield intuition was not logical reasoning ; it was a feeling. On the battlefield, Sakumo could « feel » the direction of danger, « feel » what his opponent would do next, and « feel » when to advance and when to retreat. This feeling didn’t appear out of thin air ; it was a reflex carved into his neural pathways by countless life-or-death moments over decades of actual combat.

Shuichi could understand how Sakumo’s battlefield intuition worked, but he couldn’t replicate it because intuition required real combat to validate. Sakumo’s intuition was : « If the opponent’s Chakra fluctuations occur at this frequency, there is a high probability they are about to release a large-scale Ninjutsu. » This judgment had been validated by Sakumo in over thirty real battles, with an accuracy rate of over eighty percent. Shuichi could remember this judgment, but he hadn’t validated it himself, so to him, it was still « knowledge » rather than « intuition. »

Knowledge could turn into intuition, but it required real combat.

Shuichi took a deep breath and temporarily shelved Sakumo’s battlefield intuition module. This part needed to be slowly validated and digested in real combat in the future ; it wasn’t something that could be trained on a flat patch of ground in a pine forest.

---

Shuichi stayed on the flat ground in the pine forest for about four hours.

When he stood up, his legs were a bit numb. Having sat on the pine needles for so long, the moisture had seeped up from the ground, making his trouser legs damp. Shuichi stretched his limbs and did a few sets of footwork drills on the flat ground.

His footwork felt different than before.

Previously, Shuichi’s footwork was accumulated through three years of training—forward steps, backward steps, and footwork transitions, with every step following a rhythm he had figured out on his own. Now, with Sakumo’s combat experience layered on top, there was an extra dimension to his footwork. When stepping, Shuichi would subconsciously consider the angle of entry and the transition of his follow-up moves, and the rhythm of his steps began to synchronize with the rhythm of his blade. This sense of synchronization was new ; he had never experienced it before.

Shuichi walked a few laps on the flat ground, feeling the changes in his footwork. It wasn’t that it had become faster, but rather that it had become « right. » Previously, Shuichi’s footwork was correct, but there was a gap between being correct and being precise. Sakumo’s memory had shortened that gap.

Shuichi walked back the way he came. By the time he walked out of the Forest of Death, the sky had already grown dim. He passed through the pine forest, walked past the Training Ground, and headed home along the Naka River.

Lue circled above the riverbank, and upon seeing Shuichi, flew down and landed on his shoulder.

« Digested it ? » Lue asked.

« Digested a part of it, » Shuichi said.

Lue tilted his head and looked at him, as if trying to comprehend the meaning of those words.

« A lot, » Lue said.

« Yeah, a lot. » Shuichi looked down at his right hand. « More than I expected. »

Shuichi walked upstream along the Naka River. Lue perched on his shoulder, occasionally looking down at the path.

Shuichi was thinking about the contrast between three years and an hour.

Three years of hard training, step by step, from wild rabbits to bears, from standing postures to footwork transitions, from an ordinary person to a Chunin. Every step was walked by himself, and every bit of progress was bought with sweat and time. This path was very slow, but very steady. Because every step was his own, planted firmly, and would not collapse.

An hour of Plunder, and Sakumo’s combat experience flooded into his mind, elevating his close-quarters combat by a whole level, giving direction to his tactical judgment, and turning terrain utilization into instinct. This path was very fast, but it had hidden dangers. Because it wasn’t his own ; it was borrowed. Borrowed things needed to be digested, validated, and adjusted before they could truly become one’s own.

Plunder and hard training.

Shuichi knew he needed both. Hard training was the foundation, while Plunder was the accelerator. Without the foundation of hard training, plundered things would be like castles in the air, unusable. Without the acceleration of Plunder, relying solely on hard training, it would take three years just to reach Chunin—and those three years themselves were the result of Plunder and hard training running in parallel. Without the plundered Vitality to build a foundation, relying solely on normal training, he wouldn’t even have touched the threshold of a Genin in three years. At this rate, by the Night of the Clan Demise, he would at most be an elite Jonin, which was not enough.

But he also knew that such opportunities for Plunder were extremely rare. Sakumo’s case was an exception, not the norm. For most of the time in the future, he would still have to rely on hard training.

Only when the Third Shinobi World War arrived would his Plunder ability truly shine.
